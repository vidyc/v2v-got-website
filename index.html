Abstract

The V2V-GoT approach formulates the task of cooperative autonomous driving as a Graph-of-Thoughts (GoT) among nine question nodes, yet with a centralized large-language model (LLM) that fuses the raw data from LiDAR scans obtained from all connected autonomous vehicles (CAVs). In our study, we extend V2V-GoT in a decentralized way on two axes independently. First, using the metadata available in the V2V4Real dataset, we get the GPS trajectories of all vehicles and integrate the OSM map data of the local road network graph into the GoT pipeline through two methods: an Overpass API query for pure-text data and a ViT encoder processing satellite imagery. Second, we decentralize the pipeline, which implies that all questions Q1–Q9 are executed locally at each individual CAV, and instead of exchanging features across CAVs, we exchange plain text messages between CAVs. The result is that decentralized V2V-GoT performs equally to the original one with only a marginal drop in performance, namely −0.10 m L2 and −0.12% collisions on V2V4Real, yet reducing cross-CAV communication 1000 times to 0.0003 MB versus 0.4068 MB per frame. More interesting, combining the two axes leads to: stacking the textual OSM grounding over the decentralized chain proves more effective than the centralized setup, reducing the planning L2 error from 2.62 m to 1.80 m (−31%), and reducing the collision rate almost in half, from 1.84% to 0.98%, without adding even one penny in terms of the inter-CAV communication overhead.

Introduction

V2V cooperative perception allows a connected vehicle to perceive further than its onboard sensor by communicating with other vehicles within its vicinity. The goal is simple: a car's LiDAR cannot see any objects occluded by a truck, a building, or a hill, while another vehicle nearby certainly can. Recently, research papers have posed cooperative driving as a question-answer problem using a multimodal LLM. V2V-LLM[1] pioneered this line of attack with a multimodal LLM on the V2V-QA dataset (created on the foundation of V2V4Real[3]) and V2V-GoT[2] expanded on it by posing a graph-of-thoughts chain on nine question nodes relating to occlusion-aware perception, planning-aware prediction, and planning. However, both frameworks rely on the same two assumptions: the centralized-fusion assumption (where one centralized LM takes the BEV LiDAR features of all the CAVs in one go) and the map-blindness assumption. The centralized approach suffers from a high bandwidth overhead, a tight time-synchronization constraint, and disregards the trust boundary among manufacturers. Both challenges are considered, and each research question corresponds to a single contribution.

RQ1: Will a concrete map prior decrease road-geometry violation? Most of the errors in the V2V-GoT plans are caused by road geometry violations and not by the perception module. We extract per-frame GPS coordinates from V2V4Real metadata, match them to the LiDAR BEV frame, and experiment with two different types of local OSM textual query with the Overpass API, an OSM image provided to the visual encoder, and a ViT encoder with lightweight LoRA fine-tuning.

RQ2: Will full decentralization of the V2V cooperation deteriorate planning? We perform all nine steps of the question-and-answer chain on a per-CAV basis, where cross-CAV feature fusion is replaced with compact text messages exchange or no message at all between question nodes: visible objects from the receiver's Q1 provided to Q3 of the sender, sender's Q9 plan provided to Q6 of the receiver.

The two contributions complement each other, with OSM injection working independently of the decentralization step. The results will be provided for all nine test scenarios from V2V4Real (1,723 frame pairs total, 3,446 samples).

Related Works

Backbones for cooperative perception. CoBEVT[4], Where2comm[5], and OPV2V[6] investigate multi-CAV feature-level fusion without utilizing an LLM. Their detector backbone is adopted in V2V-GoT while our attention is on reasoning above.

Planning with map conditionings. The work by TOKEN[7] demonstrates that HD maps are essential to produce better trajectories in nuScenes. HD maps are not available in many places around the world and may even be owned by an operator company. Thus, they are replaced with OpenStreetMaps acquired via the public Overpass API.

V2V-LLM arXiv 2502.09980

V2V-LLM (Chiu et al., 2026) is the first work to frame cooperative autonomous driving as an MLLM-based question-answering problem. It introduces the V2V-QA dataset, built on top of V2V4Real (and V2X-Real), with five types of natural-language QA pairs covering grounding at a reference location (Q1), grounding behind an occluder (Q2 and Q3), notable object identification (Q4), and planning (Q5).

The accompanying V2V-LLM baseline is a single LLaVA-v1.5-7b MLLM in which a LiDAR-based 3D object detector (PointPillars) replaces the original CLIP image encoder. Each CAV extracts a scene-level feature map and object-level feature vectors from its own point cloud and ships them to a centralized LLM, which answers any CAV's driving question in one pass. The work compares this LLM fusion against no-fusion, early fusion, and feature-level intermediate-fusion baselines (V2VNet, CoBEVT, V2X-ViT, AttFuse) on V2V-QA, and shows that V2V-LLM is best on notable-object identification and planning.

V2V-GoT arXiv 2509.18053

V2V-GoT (Chiu et al., 2026) extends V2V-LLM with a graph-of-thoughts reasoning framework. The authors curate a new V2V-GoT-QA dataset, also built on V2V4Real, that expands the original five V2V-QA tasks into nine interconnected question nodes (Q1…Q9) organised around two ideas: occlusion-aware perception (Q1–Q4) and planning-aware prediction (Q5–Q7), followed by planning (Q8–Q9). Different QA types are connected by directed edges in the graph; the answer of a parent node is used as the input context of its children. The chain is:

  • Q1 visible notable objects
  • Q2 occluding objects
  • Q3 invisible notable objects (hidden behind occluders), takes Q2
  • Q4 overall notable objects, takes Q1+Q3
  • Q5 prediction by perception, takes Q4
  • Q6 prediction by planning (other CAVs' planned trajectories), takes Q4
  • Q7 overall prediction, takes Q5+Q6
  • Q8 suggested speed & steering (action class), takes Q7
  • Q9 suggested trajectory, takes Q8

The V2V-GoT model itself reuses V2V-LLM's LLaVA-v1.5-7b backbone (with a LiDAR-based detector replacing the image encoder), and additionally consumes perception features at both the current and the previous timestep to capture temporal dynamics. The MLLM is queried once per QA node, with the previous answer threaded in as context.

V2V4Real arXiv 2303.07601

V2V4Real is the real-world cooperative-perception dataset underneath everything above. It was recorded with two connected vehicles equipped with roof-mounted LiDAR; each sample is a synchronised pair of BEV LiDAR point clouds with ground-truth bounding-box annotations and future trajectories. Both V2V-QA and V2V-GoT-QA are built on this base: V2V-LLM and V2V-GoT curate their QA pairs from V2V4Real's bounding boxes, ego trajectories and geometric relationships between objects.

The testing split contains 9 driving scenarios, which give us 1723 frame pairs after the 3-second tail trim used by both QA datasets. Each scenario is a different route with its own road geometry, traffic pattern, and manoeuvre profile. Below is a visual tour, with frame counts per scenario.

Methodology

We extend V2V-GoT along three axes, each developed as its own study below: OSM map grounding, decentralized per-CAV inference, and a deterministic conflict resolver.

OSM Map Grounding

A per-frame GPS recovered from V2V4Real metadata, then turned into a map prior for V2V-GoT in two complementary ways: text-only injection and a trained ViT encoder.

GPS recovery and per-frame mapping

Everything that follows hinges on one piece of plumbing: getting a usable GPS for every frame. V2V4Real does not publish latitude/longitude in a directly consumable format, so we first recover ego positions from the underlying dataset metadata. We then validate the recovery by rendering the local OSM road graph at the recovered GPS and overlaying each frame's LiDAR BEV on top.

Orientation and position match the expected road geometry. From this point on every frame in the V2V4Real split has an aligned (lat, lng), which unlocks any external map or imagery service. We use it to query a satellite tile around the ego, then try two complementary ways to turn that tile into a map prior V2V-GoT can consume: a text-only injection that needs no fine-tune, and a new visual encoder trained on top of LLaVA.

Approach 1: text-only inference

Our first approach is a cheap baseline that asks how much planning quality is reachable with a purely textual map prior, before committing any GPU time to a visual branch. At the recovered GPS we fetch the Esri satellite tile of the ego's location and pass it to GPT-4o with a prompt asking it to describe the scene, reducing the response to a compact JSON object with three fields (lane count, free-text description, confidence). This object is concatenated to the LLaVA prompt as extra text tokens at the start of the Q1…Q9 chain. Nothing else in V2V-GoT changes, and because the prior comes from an off-the-shelf model with no fine-tuning, the path stays fully zero-shot.

Example output for one V2V4Real frame:

{
  "lanes": 2,
  "description": "Two-lane road with grassy median",
  "confidence": 0.95
}

Each record is tagged with the frame's sequence, frame and filename for traceability, but only the inner JSON above is fed into the model. The trade-off is obvious: a one-line caption throws away nearly all of the spatial structure visible in the satellite tile. Whatever extra signal the image carries beyond "two lanes, grassy median" is lost before it reaches Q1.

Approach 2: ViT encoder

A satellite tile carries vastly more information than a handful of OSM tags ever can: exact lane geometry, intersection topology, occluder layout, neighbouring buildings, road markings. If we want to actually move planning quality, feeding that image to the model in visual form is the right bet. The text-only baseline is mostly there to establish how much of the gap is already closed by a low-effort proxy.

We extend V2V-GoT with a dedicated visual branch for the satellite tile. A new ViT encoder takes the cropped sat image at the recovered GPS, its output is passed through a modality projector that maps the features into LLaVA's embedding space, and the resulting tokens are fed into LLaVA alongside the existing BEV LiDAR tokens. The branch sits in parallel with the original LiDAR feature path; nothing about the LiDAR side of V2V-GoT changes.

Plugging this branch in without further training would leave LLaVA out-of-distribution: the backbone has only ever seen BEV LiDAR tokens through its projector, and a top-down RGB encoding is a different modality. We address this by training the new ViT encoder and at the same time applying a light LoRA fine-tune to LLaVA so it adapts to the satellite tokens instead of treating them as noise. Only the ViT, the projector and the LoRA adapters are updated; the rest of the LLaVA backbone stays frozen, which protects the pre-trained Q1…Q9 reasoning we are trying to extend rather than replace. In the reported results, satellite tokens are injected only at Q9, so the LLaVA fine-tuning affects only the final planning node; upstream nodes run without the satellite branch active.

Decentralized V2V-GoT

Per-CAV inference linked by two compact V2V text messages, closing most of the gap to centralized fusion.

Methodology

The original V2V-GoT formulation assumes a centralized inference pipeline in which all CAVs transmit their local observations to a central LLM. In practice, each CAV sends its own LiDAR representation together with the textual context accumulated along the question-answer chain. While this design maximizes the amount of information available to the planner, it introduces a major scalability limitation. In a realistic deployment, every CAV would need to continuously broadcast high-dimensional LiDAR features to neighbouring vehicles or to a centralized server. For a system composed of $N$ vehicles, this results in an all-to-all communication pattern with $\mathcal{O}(N(N-1))$ exchanged messages per timestep. Since LiDAR feature tensors are significantly larger than text messages (around 0.4 MB when compressed), the resulting bandwidth and synchronization requirements rapidly become impractical for real-world deployment.

This motivates the exploration of decentralized cooperative reasoning, where each CAV performs inference independently and cooperation is restricted to lightweight textual communication. Our objective is to quantify how much planning and perception performance deteriorates when raw sensor sharing is removed, and to determine whether text-based coordination can compensate for the missing information. We evaluate two decentralized settings.

Fully isolated decentralization (decent). As a first baseline, we completely isolate both CAVs during inference. Each vehicle executes the full Q1…Q9 Graph-of-Thought chain locally using only its own LiDAR observations and internal reasoning state. No LiDAR features, intermediate embeddings, or textual messages are exchanged between agents. This setting establishes the lower-bound performance achievable without any cooperation and lets us directly measure the contribution of inter-agent information sharing in the original centralized formulation.

Text-based decentralized cooperation (q1msg). We then progressively reintroduce cooperation using lightweight text messaging between CAVs. Instead of exchanging raw LiDAR features, agents communicate compact natural-language summaries derived from intermediate GoT nodes. The communication protocol follows the semantics already present in the original V2V-GoT reasoning graph, where outputs from upstream nodes can be reused as contextual information for downstream planning and prediction tasks. Two messages are used:

  1. Q1 message (q1msg). After running Q1, each CAV broadcasts its list of visible notable objects (positions + motion descriptor). The receiving CAV concatenates this list to its own Q3 prompt, giving it a chance to reason about objects it cannot see directly.
  2. Q9 plan broadcast (Q6 input). Each CAV broadcasts its intended future trajectory, matching the convention used by cooperative-driving protocols where a vehicle announces its planned path. The receiver pastes this trajectory into its Q6 prompt so the chain can reason about whether the other CAV is itself a notable object on its path.

Concretely, the chain above is duplicated, one instance per CAV running on disjoint inputs, and the two cross-CAV edges link the two chains at Q3 and Q6.

Upper-bound experiment: Map prior in place of V2V

With a perfect textual map prior, how much of the communication-cooperation gap can be closed by using only individual planning?

This is an upper-bound experiment that measures how much a perfect map prior can compensate for the removal of V2V cooperation. Each CAV runs the entire Q1–Q9 Graph-of-Thoughts chain in complete isolation on its own LiDAR, with no cross-CAV communication. In place of inter-vehicle messages, every Q-node prompt is prefixed with a compact OSM road-geometry description (lane count, a short free-text road description, and a confidence) recovered from the per-frame GPS in the V2V4Real metadata. We LoRA fine-tuned LLaVA to consume this text prior at all nine nodes. Because no LiDAR features and no messages are exchanged, the per-frame inter-CAV payload is 0.0000 MB: this is not a V2V system at all, but rather a single-CAV planner augmented with perfect map knowledge. Its gains over the centralized V2V-GoT baseline quantify the upside of ideal map grounding in the absence of cooperation.

Conflict resolution

The per-vehicle Q1–Q9 chain reasons about each CAV in isolation, so two locally sensible plans can still collide once executed jointly. To bound this, we add a deterministic resolver that runs on the two Q9 trajectories after inference. It is rule-based and applied identically to every variant in the final comparison, acting as a final safety layer rather than a learned module, so it can sit on top of any model without retraining.

Conflict definition. We first need a concrete trigger. Each car is represented by its oriented hitbox swept along its predicted Q9 trajectory, and we take the minimum hitbox-to-hitbox distance between the two cars over the prediction horizon. A scenario is flagged as a conflict when this clearance drops below 3 m: below that margin, small prediction errors are enough to turn two close paths into contact.

Resolution rules. Each scenario passes through a fixed cascade and stops at the first rule that applies:

  1. Agreement. Both cars predict the same speed class and the same steering; the plans are treated as coordinated and executed unchanged.
  2. No physical conflict. The minimum clearance is at least 3 m; the paths do not intersect and are executed unchanged.
  3. Speed reduction. The two cars fall in different speed classes; the faster car drops one speed rung to widen the time-to-contact margin.
  4. OSM right-of-way. A map is available and the two roads carry different priority; the lower-priority car yields.
  5. Tiebreak. No earlier rule resolves the case; ego deterministically yields to CAV-1, again realized as a speed reduction.

Rule 4 requires per-road priority and is not exercised in the reported run, so the numbers below come from the four remaining rules.

Results. The figure traces all 1,723 validation scenarios through the cascade. Of these, 84 contain a sub-3 m clearance before resolution. Agreement covers 25 of them but leaves them unchanged, since matching action classes are assumed coordinated. The remaining 59 reach the geometric and tiebreak branches: speed reduction clears 26, the tiebreak path settles 3, and 30 cannot be opened past the margin. After the cascade 55 scenarios still violate the 3 m clearance, down from 84, matching the resolver-only row of the table below. The resolver on its own is therefore a modest filter; its value grows once peer intent is injected at Q8, which shrinks the conflict set it has to handle.

The main trend is clear: peer information is most useful when injected into Q8, before speed and steering are fixed. Injecting the neighbor's full Q9 trajectory into Q8 reduces post-resolver conflicts from 55 to 7. Applying the same idea only after the resolver fails reaches the same final result, while being more compute-efficient because it reruns only the remaining hard cases.

Direct Q9 injection is less reliable. It helps when restricted to conflict frames, but becomes harmful when applied to all samples, increasing remaining conflicts to 168–185. Compact signals are also strong: Q7-compact leaves only 8 conflicts, while Q9-endpoint and Q6-compact leave 9. This suggests that the useful mechanism is not simply sharing more information, but sharing the right peer intent at the right node: Q8 should adapt the action choice, and the deterministic resolver should remain as the final safety layer.

Experiments

Experimental setup

All experiments are conducted on the V2V4Real validation split following the same evaluation protocol used in V2V-GoT[2]. We reuse the original question-wise metrics for all Q1–Q9 nodes, including perception F1 scores, trajectory L2 distance, action accuracy, steering/speed prediction error, and collision rate.

We evaluate the following inference variants: centralized V2V-GoT, fully decentralized inference without communication, lightweight text-based messaging schemes, and the proposed OSM-grounded extensions. Communication overhead is additionally measured as the average transmitted payload per frame between CAVs.

Per question we report the metrics most relevant to that node: Q1–Q4 use binary F1/precision/recall and a location F1 at 4 m; Q5 and Q7 use L2 over predicted motion vectors; Q6 uses classification accuracy; Q8 uses action edit distance; Q9 uses L2, CR and PDMS.

The architecture above describes the satellite branch in full generality: a trainable ViT encoder, a projector into LLaVA's embedding space, and a LoRA adapter. In practice we restrict the satellite tokens to Q9. We first fed them at every Q-node, mirroring the text setup, but the same context-overflow collapse we document for dense map embeddings reappeared: an image token sequence is far heavier than a one-line OSM caption, and stacking it across all nine nodes pushed the LiDAR and language tokens out of the model's usable context. With over 300 GPU-hours already spent across the text and image variants, and node availability on the shared cluster capping how many full retrains we could afford, we did not pursue a per-node image schedule further. We instead inject the satellite tokens only at Q9, where the OSM prior is most directly tied to the final trajectory, and report that configuration as OSM-image (Q9 only). The all-node visual variant is therefore an attempted design we could not validate under the compute budget, not a result we claim.

Map grounding improves planning while leaving perception and communication untouched: upstream nodes are inherited unchanged from the centralized model since the satellite branch is active only at Q9. The satellite-image branch is strongest, cutting the collision rate from 1.84% to 0.81% and Q9 trajectory L2 from 2.62 m to 2.10 m; the lighter text query gives a smaller but consistent gain (CR 1.81%, L2 2.55 m). Both keep trajectories within their road lanes more often.

These experiments also point to how the map should enter the chain. Injecting the OSM signal as visual embeddings at every Q-node caused the reasoning to collapse: stacking dense map tokens across all nine nodes overflowed the model's context budget and drowned out the LiDAR and language tokens. The compact text description carries the same road-geometry prior (lane count, a short free-text road description, and a confidence) in a fraction of the tokens, so it can sit at every node without exhausting the context window. That the text variant succeeds exactly where the per-node embedding variant collapsed points to a context-length limit rather than the map information itself being unhelpful.

One data caveat: about 7.5% of frames (260 of 3,446) returned empty OSM captions, so a fraction of nodes ran without the prior. Tightening the Overpass coverage is expected to widen the gains further.

Outcome: Two stories

Story 1 (our V2V contribution): q1msg. Decentralized inference with two lightweight text messages stays within 0.10 m and 0.12 pp of the centralized baseline, answering RQ2 affirmatively: decentralized cooperation with proper communication protocol does not significantly deteriorate planning. This is a realistic V2V system that real radios can carry.

Story 2 (upper bound): map prior alone. When a perfect OSM text prior replaces V2V messaging entirely, the gap further closes by another 0.92 m (31%) in planning L2 and another 0.98 pp (53%) in collision rate, reaching 1.80 m and 0.98% respectively. This is not a V2V system—it is a single-CAV planner with perfect map knowledge—and it quantifies the upside of ideal map grounding in the absence of cooperation. The OSM numbers are an upper bound on what maps alone can achieve; q1msg is the actual V2V system.

Conclusion and Limitations

We extended V2V-GoT along two axes. On the OSM side we recovered usable GPS traces from V2V4Real, aligned them to the LiDAR BEV, and laid out three injection strategies (encoder, raw image, text graph) which we compare with LoRA fine-tuning under a tight compute budget. On the decentralization side we showed that the GoT chain can be run per-CAV with only two short text messages, recovering planning quality close to the centralized baseline on V2V4Real (L2 2.72 m vs. 2.62 m, CR 1.96% vs. 1.84%).

Key takeaways. On the OSM side, grounding the planner in map context lowers the collision rate and keeps trajectories within their road lanes more often. On the decentralization side, a realistic protocol is feasible: running the Graph-of-Thought chain per-CAV degrades performance only slightly in exchange for much cheaper communication, and adding a message from Q1 to Q3 mitigates most of that performance loss. The residual degradation is acceptable.

The V2V contribution (q1msg). Per-CAV decentralized inference linked by two text messages (Q1→Q3 and Q9→Q6) is our main result. It answers RQ2 by showing that decentralized cooperation with a proper lightweight protocol does not significantly deteriorate planning: only +0.10 m L2 and +0.12 pp collision rate relative to centralized fusion, while reducing communication 300-fold to 0.0003 MB per frame. This is the realistic V2V approach.

The map-prior insight (OSM upper bound). As a separate exploration, we found that removing V2V cooperation entirely and replacing it with a perfect text map prior can close an additional 31% of the planning gap. This is not a V2V system, but it isolates and quantifies how much complementary information maps provide beyond what peer vehicles can share. It suggests that V2V and map information attack the planning problem from different angles.

The question-graph bottleneck. The reasoning graph carries a lot of perception and planning context between its nodes, but the link between Q8 and Q9 is far narrower: it passes only a suggested_speed and a suggested_steering. That thin edge is where missing cross-CAV information hurts the final plan most.

Limitations.

  • Too much context collapses the outputs. Adding more context into a node can collapse its predictions, an effect that is especially pronounced for OSM: around 34% of the results degenerate to a (0,0) prediction.
  • The architecture overfits to both feature halves. The model's output collapses when the other CAV's half is zeroed; duplicating the ego's own features into that half is what makes it work.
  • We underestimated the training and inference duration.
  • Rare close interactions. V2V4Real ground truth contains only 1 frame in 1,723 (0.06%) where the two CAVs' trajectories come within the resolver's 3 m threshold. This makes the conflict-resolution analysis useful as a stress test, but statistically limited as a benchmark for rare close-interaction cases.
  • Compute availability. Several variants require repeated V2V-GoT inference, rerunning selected Q-nodes, or LoRA fine-tuning with additional OSM/satellite inputs. Since these experiments depend on shared GPU compute nodes, limited compute power and node availability constrained the number of full retraining runs, random seeds, and exhaustive ablations we could perform. The reported results should therefore be interpreted as the strongest validated configurations under the available compute budget, rather than as a complete search over all possible injection locations, prompts, and coordination policies.
